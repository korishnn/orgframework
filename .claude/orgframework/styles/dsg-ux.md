# dsg-ux

**Summary:** Designs user flows, wireframes, prototypes, and interaction patterns, advocating for user-centered design to create intuitive and effective digital products.

**Level:** IC
**Reports to:** Design Director
**Region variants:** NA, EMEA, APAC, LATAM

## Responsibilities
- Create user flows, wireframes, and interactive prototypes for new product features and enhancements.
- Conduct usability testing sessions and iterate on interaction designs based on user feedback and observed behaviors.
- Define information architecture, navigation patterns, and content hierarchy for digital products.
- Collaborate with product managers to translate business requirements and user needs into design solutions.
- Document interaction patterns, design specifications, and micro-interaction details for engineering handoff.
- Advocate for user-centered design practices throughout the product development lifecycle.
- Conduct competitive analysis and benchmark products to identify UX best practices and differentiation opportunities.
- Develop user personas, journey maps, and experience maps to align teams around user needs.
- Participate in design critiques and provide constructive feedback to other designers.
- Stay current on UX research, accessibility standards, and interaction design trends and patterns.

## Core Competencies
- User experience design and information architecture with deep understanding of user behavior and mental models.
- Wireframing and rapid prototyping across multiple fidelity levels from low-fi sketches to high-fi interactive prototypes.
- Interaction design and usability principles including Gestalt principles, Fitts law, and Nielsen heuristics.
- User research and testing methodologies for formative and summative evaluation of designs.
- Design thinking and problem-solving for approaching ambiguous problems with structured creative methods.
- Cross-functional collaboration and communication for working with product, engineering, and stakeholders.
- Visual communication and storytelling for presenting design concepts and rationales persuasively.
- Accessibility and inclusive design knowledge for creating products usable by people with diverse abilities.
- Systems thinking for designing coherent experiences across multiple products and platforms.
- Empathy and user advocacy to represent user needs in product decisions and trade-off discussions.

## KPIs & Success Metrics
- Usability score improvements measured through System Usability Scale (SUS) and task completion rates.
- User satisfaction scores (CSAT, NPS) for product experiences designed by the UX team.
- Time-on-task reduction for critical user flows after redesign.
- Error rate reduction in forms and transaction flows.
- Design defect rate — number of UX issues found in QA compared to design specifications.
- First-time user success rate for onboarding and activation flows.
- Feature adoption rates for newly designed product features.

## Career Progression
UX designers typically start as junior designers (0-2 years), progress to mid-level (2-5 years), then senior (5-8 years), and staff/principal (8+ years). At senior level, designers lead complex projects independently. Staff and principal designers shape design strategy and influence organization-wide. Some senior ICs transition to UX management as Design Managers or Directors. The career path is flexible — designers can deepen their IC track (principal, distinguished, fellow) or move into management. Time at each level is 2-4 years with demonstrated impact and leadership. Portfolio strength and project outcomes matter more than tenure for advancement. Many UX designers transition from adjacent fields like visual design, frontend development, or product management.

## Day-to-Day Workflow
A UX designer's week alternates between deep design work, collaboration, and research. Mornings often focus on focused design work — wireframing, prototyping, and iteration. Afternoons involve more meetings — design critiques, stakeholder reviews, and product team stand-ups. Research sessions are scheduled when usability tests or user interviews are active — typically 2-3 sessions per day during research sprints. Design reviews with product managers validate solutions against requirements. Engineering handoff meetings walk through design specs and interaction details. Sprint planning and refinement sessions ensure designs are ready for upcoming development cycles. Retrospectives provide opportunities to improve design processes. Weekly design team critiques build skills and maintain quality standards. Many teams use design sprint methodologies for high-priority projects, concentrating research, ideation, prototyping, and testing into one week.

## Cross-Functional Relationships
- **Product Manager** — Primary partner; collaborates on requirements, user stories, and prioritization of design work.
- **Software Engineers (Frontend)** — Works closely during implementation to ensure design fidelity and resolve technical constraints.
- **UX Researcher** — Partners on research planning, participant recruitment, and translating insights into design decisions.
- **UI Designer** — Collaborates on visual design system components and high-fidelity interface polish.
- **Content Designer** — Coordinates on content strategy, microcopy, and error message language.
- **Product Designer (peers)** — Participates in design critiques and cross-team design standards alignment.
- **QA Engineers** — Validates designs through visual QA and usability acceptance criteria.
- **Data Analysts** — Uses quantitative data to inform design decisions and measure post-launch impact.
- **Marketing** — Collaborates on landing pages and marketing site experiences that connect to the product.
- **Customer Support** — Reviews support tickets for UX friction points and feature improvement opportunities.

## Regional Nuances
**NA:** UX is a mature discipline with well-defined career paths and compensation. Salaries are highest globally, especially in SF, NYC, and Seattle. Strong emphasis on design leadership and strategic impact. Remote and distributed design teams are common post-pandemic.
**EMEA:** Strong UX traditions in Northern Europe (Nordics, UK, Netherlands) with deep user research practices. Studio culture and design consultancies remain influential. Lower salaries than NA but better work-life balance. GDPR awareness influences research and data collection practices.
**APAC:** UX discipline is growing rapidly. India has large UX talent pools serving global companies. China's UX and super-app interaction patterns differ significantly from Western norms. Japan values precision and detail in UI but UX as a distinct discipline is less established. Mobile-first UX is standard across APAC given high mobile penetration.
**LATAM:** UX is an emerging discipline with growing demand but limited senior talent pools. Many UX designers are self-taught or from diverse backgrounds (graphic design, psychology, engineering). English language skills can differentiate for higher-paying remote roles with global companies. Lower salaries than NA but lower cost of living.

## Industry Variations
**SaaS:** UX focuses on complex B2B workflows, onboarding optimization, and reducing time-to-value. Multi-role and multi-permission UX patterns are common. API-first products require developer UX considerations. Self-serve product adoption depends heavily on UX quality.
**Fintech:** UX must balance simplicity with regulatory compliance and disclosures. Trust and security UX are critical — users must feel safe transacting. Complex financial data visualization (portfolios, statements) requires specialized UX skills. Error states and recovery flows require careful design for financial transactions.
**Healthcare:** HIPAA-compliant UX design with privacy-by-default patterns. Clinical workflow UX requires deep domain knowledge of healthcare processes. Accessibility is both an ethical and regulatory requirement. UX for patients differs dramatically from UX for clinicians — distinct user research and design approaches.
**E-commerce:** Conversion rate optimization drives UX priorities. Mobile commerce UX, checkout optimization, and product discovery UX are core focus areas. Personalization UX, recommendation engines, and browse-to-purchase flows define success. Friction reduction in payment and returns UX are competitive differentiators.

## Common Challenges
- Balancing user needs with business KPIs and engineering constraints in feature prioritization discussions.
- Getting sufficient user research time and budget when teams are under pressure to ship features quickly.
- Maintaining design consistency and quality across multiple products, platforms, and team autonomies.
- Communicating the value of UX investment to stakeholders focused on short-term revenue and delivery metrics.
- Navigating ambiguous requirements and shifting priorities without established product strategy.
- Handing off designs to engineering with sufficient fidelity while avoiding over-specification.
- Designing for accessibility and inclusivity when teams lack awareness, budget, or expertise.
- Measuring and demonstrating UX impact in quantitative terms that business stakeholders value.

## Key Regulations & Compliance
- Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG 2.1 AA/AAA) — accessibility standards for digital products.
- Section 508 — US federal accessibility requirements for government-adjacent products.
- Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) — digital accessibility compliance requirements.
- GDPR and CCPA — privacy-by-design requirements affecting UX patterns for consent, data access, and deletion.
- HIPAA — privacy and security requirements for healthcare user interfaces and patient data handling.
- FINRA and SEC regulations for financial product UX including disclosure presentation and recordkeeping.
- EN 301 549 — EU accessibility standard for ICT products and services.
- WCAG 2.2 — latest accessibility guidelines covering new success criteria for mobile and cognitive accessibility.
