# sec-forensics

**Summary:** Digital Forensics Analyst who conducts forensic investigations, evidence collection, and chain-of-custody documentation for internal investigations and legal proceedings.

**Level:** IC
**Reports to:** CISO / Incident Response Director
**Region variants:** NA, EMEA, APAC, LATAM

## Responsibilities
- Collect and preserve digital evidence following forensic best practices to maintain integrity and admissibility.
- Conduct in-depth forensic analysis of compromised systems, network logs, storage media, and cloud environments.
- Maintain chain-of-custody documentation for all evidence collected during investigations.
- Produce comprehensive forensic reports for internal stakeholders, legal teams, and law enforcement.
- Support incident response with real-time forensic data collection during active security incidents.
- Identify root cause and attacker methodology through artifact analysis and timeline reconstruction.
- Recover deleted, hidden, or encrypted data from storage media and systems.
- Conduct memory forensics to identify malware, rootkits, and in-memory artifacts.
- Perform mobile device forensics for investigations involving smartphones and tablets.
- Provide expert testimony in legal proceedings based on forensic findings and analysis.
- Develop and maintain forensic analysis procedures, playbooks, and tool configurations.
- Stay current with forensic methodologies, anti-forensic techniques, and evolving digital environments.

## Core Competencies
- Digital forensics methodologies (NIST SP 800-86, SANS FOR series, ACPO principles).
- Memory and disk forensics tools and techniques for deep system analysis.
- Log analysis and timeline reconstruction for understanding attack sequences and user actions.
- Chain-of-custody procedures and evidence handling for legal admissibility of digital evidence.
- Report writing for both technical and legal audiences with clear, defensible findings.
- Expert testimony preparation and presentation skills for legal proceedings.
- File system forensics for NTFS, EXT, HFS+, APFS, and modern filesystems.
- Network forensics for analyzing traffic captures, logs, and network artifacts.
- Cloud and container forensics for AWS, GCP, Azure, Docker, and Kubernetes environments.
- Mobile device forensics for iOS and Android device acquisition and analysis.
- Malware analysis and reverse engineering fundamentals for understanding malicious code.
- Anti-forensic detection for identifying evidence tampering, wiping, or destruction attempts.
- Forensic tool proficiency across commercial and open-source tools.
- Evidence acquisition from live systems, dead boxes, and remote/cloud environments.

## KPIs & Success Metrics
- Case resolution time — average time from evidence collection to forensic report delivery.
- Evidence integrity rate — percentage of cases with complete, unbroken chain-of-custody.
- Forensic finding accuracy — percentage of findings confirmed through independent validation.
- Court admissibility rate — percentage of cases where forensic evidence is admitted in legal proceedings.
- Backlog clearance — number of pending forensic cases awaiting analysis.
- Recovery success rate — percentage of requested data recovery cases yielding usable evidence.
- Mean time to root cause identification during active incident forensic support.

## Career Progression
Digital forensics professionals typically start as junior forensic analysts (0-2 years), progress to forensic analysts (2-5 years), senior forensic analysts (5-8 years), and lead/principal forensic analysts or forensic director (8+ years). Growth involves expanding from tool-based analysis to complex multi-platform investigations and expert testimony. Senior analysts handle the most complex cases, develop new forensic techniques, and provide expert testimony. Career branching includes incident response specialization, e-discovery, forensic research (tool development, anti-forensics), or law enforcement. Many forensic professionals come from law enforcement, military intelligence, or IT security backgrounds. Certifications are critical (GCFA, GCFE, GNFA, EnCE, CCFP, CCE). Court qualification as an expert witness distinguishes senior practitioners. Private sector forensics often pays better than law enforcement but may lack the same court exposure.

## Day-to-Day Workflow
A forensic analyst's day is driven by active cases and incoming requests. Case intake involves scoping the request, defining collection parameters, and obtaining legal authorization (if required). Evidence collection can be remote (network-based acquisition) or onsite (physical media acquisition). Analysis is the most time-consuming phase — examining disk images, memory dumps, network logs, and application artifacts to answer investigative questions. Analysis follows a structured approach: evidence verification, keyword searching, timeline analysis, artifact examination, and correlation. Documentation is continuous throughout the case — detailed notes on analysis steps, findings, and chain-of-custody. Report writing synthesizes findings into clear, defensible reports suitable for the audience (HR, legal, executive, law enforcement). Consultation with legal teams on evidence admissibility, discovery obligations, and expert testimony preparation. Between cases, tool evaluation, methodology development, and lab maintenance fill the time. Professional development through new tool training, certification maintenance, and forensic conference attendance.

## Cross-Functional Relationships
- **Incident Response Team** — Primary partner during active incidents; provides real-time forensic support and evidence collection.
- **SOC Team** — Receives escalated cases requiring deep forensic analysis beyond initial triage.
- **Legal / Litigation** — Primary stakeholder for forensic evidence in legal proceedings; coordinates on discovery and testimony.
- **HR** — Partners on employee investigations (policy violations, misconduct, insider threat).
- **Law Enforcement** — Coordinates on criminal cases requiring digital evidence; provides evidence in admissible formats.
- **Internal Audit** — Supports forensic data collection for audit investigations and fraud examinations.
- **IT Operations** — Arranges system access, evidence acquisition windows, and preservation of relevant systems.
- **e-Discovery Team** - Coordinates on electronic discovery processes and evidence preservation for litigation hold.
- **Data Privacy** - Consults on evidence handling involving personal data and privacy compliance.
- **External Forensic Firms** - Coordinates on overflow cases, specialized expertise, or independent validation.
- **Engineering** - Consults on application-specific artifact analysis and custom system forensics.
- **Executive Management** - Provides briefings on investigation findings and business impact.

## Regional Nuances
**NA:** Well-established forensics discipline with certified practitioners. Courts have extensive experience with digital evidence admissibility standards (Daubert, Frye). Forensic labs exist in major corporations, law enforcement agencies (FBI RCFL, Secret Service), and consulting firms. Strong emphasis on certification and expert witness qualification.
**EMEA:** GDPR creates tension between forensic investigation and data subject rights — evidence preservation can conflict with right to deletion. UK ACPO principles (Association of Chief Police Officers) guide digital evidence handling. EU Data Protection Authorities may need to be consulted for forensic investigations involving employee data. Varying legal standards for evidence admissibility across EU countries. Works Council notification for employee device forensic examination.
**APAC:** Digital forensics maturity varies significantly across the region. Singapore and Australia have well-developed forensic capabilities and legal frameworks. China has its own forensic standards and tool ecosystem. India's IT Act provides legal framework for digital evidence, but forensic maturity varies. Japan's forensic investigation culture emphasizes process and documentation. Cross-border data access restrictions complicate multi-national investigations.
**LATAM:** Digital forensics is a developing field with growing professionalization. Brazil has emerging forensic capabilities and legal recognition of digital evidence. Forensic tool access can be limited by budget and import restrictions. Growing cybercrime drives demand for forensic services. Limited expert witness pools for court proceedings. Collaboration with international law enforcement is common for major cases.

## Industry Variations
**SaaS:** Forensics focuses on cloud evidence collection (API logs, cloud trail, access logs), container forensics (ephemeral evidence preservation), and source code theft investigations. Multi-tenant environment forensics requires isolation considerations. SaaS application log analysis for abuse and account compromise investigations.
**Fintech:** Financial fraud investigations require transaction record forensics and trading system analysis. Regulatory investigations (SEC, FINRA) require defensible forensic processes. Payment system forensics for card data breaches (PCI forensic investigation). Insider trading investigations with complex electronic communication analysis. Money laundering and financial crime forensic support.
**Healthcare:** Medical device forensics for investigating device compromise or data theft. EHR system access forensics for privacy breach investigations. Patient data exfiltration investigations with PHI handling sensitivity. Clinical research data integrity investigations. Regulatory investigations (HHS OCR, FDA) with strict evidence requirements.
**E-commerce:** Payment card data breach forensics with PCI forensic investigator (PFI) requirements. Account takeover investigations requiring customer account activity forensics. Intellectual property theft investigations for product and pricing data. Large-scale fraud investigation forensics during holiday season incidents. Supply chain compromise investigations for third-party system access.

## Common Challenges
- Keeping forensic skills and tools current with rapidly evolving operating systems, cloud environments, and anti-forensic techniques.
- Acquiring forensic evidence from ephemeral cloud and container environments where data disappears after termination.
- Managing the forensic caseload when the volume of investigations exceeds analyst capacity.
- Handling encrypted data and devices where suspect cooperation or lawful decryption may not be available.
- Preserving evidence across distributed, global infrastructure with different legal regimes and data protection laws.
- Maintaining chain-of-custody when evidence must pass through multiple teams, jurisdictions, or organizations.
- Providing clear, actionable forensic findings to non-technical stakeholders (HR, legal, management) without oversimplifying.
- Navigating the tension between thorough forensic analysis and the urgency of providing findings for time-sensitive decisions.

## Key Regulations & Compliance
- NIST SP 800-86 — guide to integrating forensic techniques into incident response methodology.
- ACPO Principles — UK digital evidence handling principles for admissibility in legal proceedings.
- Federal Rules of Evidence (US) — rules governing admissibility of digital evidence in federal courts.
- Daubert Standard — court admissibility standard for expert witness testimony and forensic methodology.
- HIPAA — forensic evidence handling of PHI with privacy safeguards.
- GDPR — forensic evidence collection involving personal data must comply with data protection principles.
- PCI DSS Requirements 10 and 12 — forensic investigation requirements for cardholder data breaches.
- Sarbanes-Oxley — evidence preservation requirements during financial investigations.
- e-Discovery (Federal Rules of Civil Procedure) — electronic discovery obligations for legal proceedings.
- Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — legal framework for forensic investigation authorization.
- Data retention laws — evidence preservation obligations under various regulatory regimes.
- Cross-border data access laws (CLOUD Act, GDPR restrictions) — legal frameworks for accessing evidence across jurisdictions.
- ISO/IEC 27037 — guidelines for identification, collection, acquisition, and preservation of digital evidence.
- Computer Misuse Act (UK, Singapore, Malaysia) — legal frameworks for computer forensics authorization.
