# sec-threat

**Summary:** Researches threat actors, attack patterns, and emerging vulnerabilities, providing intelligence that drives proactive defense and detection improvements.

**Level:** IC
**Reports to:** CISO
**Region variants:** NA, EMEA, APAC, LATAM

## Responsibilities
- Research and analyze threat actors, their motivations, capabilities, tactics, techniques, and procedures (TTPs).
- Monitor threat intelligence feeds, open-source intelligence (OSINT), dark web sources, and industry sharing groups.
- Produce threat intelligence reports, briefings, adversary profiles, and situational awareness updates.
- Identify emerging vulnerabilities and zero-days, assess their potential impact on the organization.
- Contribute detection signatures, indicators of compromise (IOCs), and detection rules to security monitoring tools.
- Collaborate with industry peers through information sharing communities (ISACs, ISAOs).
- Conduct intelligence-driven threat hunting to identify undetected compromises.
- Maintain the threat intelligence platform and intelligence lifecycle from requirements to feedback.
- Develop threat models for the organization's specific industry, technology stack, and threat profile.
- Provide intelligence context to incident response teams during active investigations.
- Evaluate new intelligence sources, tools, and analytical techniques for program improvement.
- Educate stakeholders on the threat landscape and intelligence-driven security practices.

## Core Competencies
- Threat intelligence analysis and research using structured analytical techniques (SATs) and critical thinking.
- Cyber threat landscape knowledge spanning APT groups, cybercriminal ecosystems, hacktivism, and insider threats.
- Intelligence tradecraft and analytical methodologies including the intelligence cycle and the DIAMOND model.
- Technical analysis of malware, exploits, and attack infrastructure for understanding adversary capabilities.
- Written and verbal intelligence reporting for producing actionable intelligence for diverse audiences.
- Open-source intelligence (OSINT) gathering using systematic collection and verification methodologies.
- Network and host-based forensics for extracting indicators from compromised systems.
- Programming and scripting for intelligence automation and data processing (Python, SQL).
- Threat modeling frameworks (STRIDE, PASTA, ATT&CK-based threat modeling).
- Geopolitical awareness for understanding the strategic context of cyber threats.
- Information security fundamentals across network, endpoint, cloud, and application security.
- Intelligence sharing best practices and handling of sensitive threat intelligence (TLP classification).

## KPIs & Success Metrics
- Intelligence actionability — percentage of threat intelligence reports that lead to detection changes, preventative controls, or response improvements.
- Intelligence timeliness — time from threat emergence to internal intelligence dissemination.
- Detection uplift — number of new detection rules or signatures created from intelligence.
- Intelligence source value — assessment of which intelligence sources provide the highest-value actionable intelligence.
- Threat hunting leads converted to confirmed findings.
- Stakeholder satisfaction with intelligence quality, relevance, and timeliness (survey score).
- Intelligence community participation — contributions to ISAC/ISAO sharing groups.
- Mean time to intelligence-informed pivot — speed of adjusting defenses based on new intelligence.

## Career Progression
Threat intelligence analysts typically start as junior analysts (0-2 years), progress to intelligence analysts (2-5 years), senior analysts (5-8 years), and lead/principal analysts (8+ years). Growth involves expanding from tactical intelligence (IOCs) to operational (TTPs) and strategic (threat actor motivations, geopolitical context) intelligence. Senior analysts lead intelligence programs, develop threat actor profiles, and brief executive leadership. Career branching includes offensive security (red teaming), threat hunting, or security research. Many threat intelligence professionals come from military intelligence, law enforcement, or national security backgrounds. Certifications (GCTI, GCIH, CISSP, CTIA) are valued. Language skills (Russian, Chinese, Korean, Farsi, Arabic) differentiate senior analysts. Advanced degrees in security studies or international relations are common.

## Day-to-Day Workflow
A threat intelligence analyst's day begins with monitoring intelligence feeds, reviewing overnight alerts, and updating the team on emerging threats. Daily intelligence processing involves triaging alerts, validating IOCs, and assessing relevance to the organization. Intelligence production includes writing threat advisories, updating adversary profiles, and creating briefings for different audiences (technical teams, management, executives). Threat hunting activities use intelligence to develop hypotheses and search for compromise indicators in the environment. Collaboration with SOC and IR teams provides intelligence context during active investigations. Intelligence source evaluation assesses new feeds, tools, and information sharing opportunities. Community participation involves ISAC meetings, threat intelligence sharing calls, and peer group collaboration. Strategic intelligence work researches long-term trends, geopolitical factors, and evolving adversary capabilities. Weekly team intelligence briefings share findings and coordinate production. Quarterly threat landscape reports provide strategic intelligence to leadership.

## Cross-Functional Relationships
- **SOC Team** — Primary consumer of tactical intelligence for detection rule tuning and alert prioritization.
- **Incident Response** — Provides intelligence context during investigations and threat actor attribution.
- **Security Engineering** — Informs security control priorities based on intelligence about relevant threats.
- **Vulnerability Management** — Prioritizes vulnerability patching based on intelligence about active exploitation.
- **Red Team** — Collaborates on realistic adversary emulation scenarios informed by threat intelligence.
- **Product Security** - Provides intelligence on threats targeting the organization's products and technology stack.
- **GRC / Risk** - Informs risk assessment with threat intelligence context about relevant threat actors.
- **Executive Leadership** - Receives strategic intelligence briefings on threat landscape and organizational exposure.
- **Legal / Compliance** - Consults on intelligence-related legal considerations and sanctions compliance.
- **External Intelligence Partners** - Participates in information sharing through ISACs, peer groups, and vendor relationships.
- **Law Enforcement** - Shares intelligence on criminal threat actors through established channels.
- **Industry Peers** - Participates in trust groups and intelligence sharing communities for mutual defense.

## Regional Nuances
**NA:** Threat intelligence is a mature discipline with specialized vendors and high demand. Intelligence focuses on APT groups from China, Russia, North Korea, and Iran, plus cybercriminal ecosystems (ransomware groups, BEC operations). CISA and FBI provide government intelligence partnerships. ISACs are well-established (FS-ISAC, IT-ISAC, REN-ISAC).
**EMEA:** Intelligence focuses on state-sponsored threats (Russia, Iran), cybercrime operations originating from Eastern Europe, and hacktivist groups aligned with geopolitical issues. ENISA provides regional threat landscape reporting. GDPR affects intelligence data handling. European intelligence sharing through ECSO and national CERTs. Language diversity requires multi-lingual intelligence capabilities.
**APAC:** Intelligence focuses on state-sponsored threats from China, North Korea, and regional actors. APAC-specific cybercriminal ecosystems and scam operations. China's APT groups are a primary focus for most programs. Cyber espionage for intellectual property theft is a major concern. Australia's ASD provides intelligence partnerships. Singapore's CSA coordinates regional threat intelligence.
**LATAM:** Threat intelligence is a developing discipline. Focus on cybercriminal groups operating in the region, ransomware, financial fraud, and politically motivated hacktivism. Government intelligence partnerships less developed than in NA/Europe. Growing organized cybercrime ecosystem in Brazil and other LATAM countries. Limited dedicated intelligence teams — often combined with other security roles.

## Industry Variations
**SaaS:** Intelligence focuses on threats targeting cloud infrastructure, SaaS applications, and customer data. Nation-state targeting of technology supply chains. Credential theft and session hijacking threats. Ransomware groups targeting SaaS backup data. Intelligence drives customer security assurance and trust programs.
**Fintech:** Intelligence focuses on financially motivated threat actors — sophisticated cybercrime groups targeting payment systems, SWIFT, and financial infrastructure. Nation-state threats to financial stability and critical financial infrastructure. Fraud intelligence integration with security intelligence. FS-ISAC participation is standard. Regulatory expectations for threat intelligence programs.
**Healthcare:** Intelligence focuses on ransomware groups targeting healthcare (patient safety risk), nation-state medical IP theft, and hacktivist groups. Ransomware intelligence is critical given patient safety implications. Medical device threat intelligence for understanding vulnerabilities in clinical systems. Health-ISAC participation. Regulatory expectations for threat awareness.
**E-commerce:** Intelligence focuses on payment card fraud, account takeover campaigns, and competitive intelligence from cybercriminal underground. Magecart and web skimming intelligence is specific to e-commerce. Seasonal threat patterns (holiday season fraud). Targeted attacks on payment processing infrastructure. Cybercriminal ecosystem monitoring for credential sales and carding forums.

## Common Challenges
- Converting intelligence from raw data and reports into actionable defensive actions that reduce risk.
- Cutting through intelligence noise — managing intelligence volume from multiple sources and focusing on organization-relevant threats.
- Keeping intelligence current with rapidly evolving threat landscapes and attacker TTP changes.
- Building trust and credibility with internal consumers who may be skeptical of intelligence quality and relevance.
- Measuring intelligence program effectiveness and demonstrating value to leadership.
- Navigating intelligence classification and sharing sensitivities (TLP controls, proprietary information).
- Balancing tactical (IOC-focused) and strategic (long-term trend) intelligence production with limited resources.
- Managing intelligence analyst burnout from exposure to continuous adversarial activity and sometimes disturbing content.

## Key Regulations & Compliance
- GDPR — intelligence data handling, particularly personal data collected through OSINT.
- CISA — US critical infrastructure threat information sharing protections and liability protections.
- Cybersecurity Information Sharing Act (CISA) — US framework for voluntary cyber threat indicator sharing.
- Anti-terrorism and sanctions regulations — compliance when tracking threat actors subject to sanctions.
- TLP (Traffic Light Protocol) — standardized classification for intelligence sharing sensitivity.
- ISAC participation rules — membership agreements, information handling, and disclosure restrictions.
- National security classification if working with government-derived intelligence.
- Privacy regulations affecting collection and use of OSINT involving personal information.
- Industry-specific information sharing protections (DHS CISA, DIB CS, FS-ISAC).
